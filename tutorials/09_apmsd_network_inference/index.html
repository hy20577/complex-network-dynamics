    Network Inference with MIR and APMSD¶

    This tutorial runs the full method of Yıldırım & Antonopoulos (2024) on the first example of the paper (Section 3.2.1): four coupled logistic maps on a chain network. The paper reports exact numbers for this case (Table 2 and Figs. 2 and 3), so every result below can be compared with it.

    The method has four steps:

    1. Compute the mutual information rate (MIR) of every pair of nodes.
    2. Generate amplitude-phase modulated surrogate data (APMSD): a fraction \(pc_{1}\) of the instantaneous amplitudes and a fraction \(pc_{2}\) of the instantaneous phases of each signal are replaced by random values.
    3. Test each pair: \(H_0\) says the two nodes are not connected. The \(p\)-value is the fraction of surrogates whose MIR is at least the MIR of the data. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (FDR) corrects for testing many pairs at once.
    4. Repeat for an \(11 \times 11\) grid of \((pc_{1}, pc_{2})\) and compare each inferred network with the true one using TPR and FPR.

    The network and the data¶

    The network is a chain of 4 nodes, \(1-2-3-4\) (Fig. 2a of the paper). Each node is a logistic map, \(f(x) = r x (1 - x)\) with \(r = 4\), coupled as in Eq. (13):

    \[ x^i_{n+1} = (1 - \alpha) f(x^i_n) + \frac{\alpha}{k_i} \sum_{j=1}^{N} A_{ij} f(x^j_n), \qquad \alpha = 0.1 . \]

    Amplitude correlation and phase synchronisation¶

    Table 1 of the paper describes this system with the mean Pearson correlation of the pairs (about 0.05) and the Kuramoto order parameter \(\rho\) of the Hilbert phases (0.93). Correlation is weak but the phases are strongly synchronised.

    To reproduce these numbers we pass demean=False to hilbert_phases, as the paper computed the phases of the raw signals. The maps take values in \([0, 1]\), and the Hilbert phase of a signal with a nonzero mean hardly moves, so part of this synchronisation is an artefact of the offset: with the library's default, demean=True, the order parameter is much lower.

    One APMSD test: (pc₁, pc₂) = (0, 60)%¶

    The paper shows this pair in Fig. 2c and Table 2: the amplitudes are kept and 60% of the phases are randomised. A significance level \(p_{sl} = 0.1\) means \(\aleph_{SD} = 1/p_{sl} = 10\) surrogates per pair, as in the paper.

    mir_surrogate_test computes the MIR of the data and of every surrogate, the \(p\)-values and the FDR correction, and returns the inferred network. Given the true network, it also returns TPR and FPR.

    The \((pc_{1}, pc_{2})\) parameter space¶

    Now repeat the test on the \(11 \times 11\) grid \(pc_{1}, pc_{2} \in \{0, 10, \dots, 100\}\%\) (Fig. 2d). Each point needs the MIR of 10 surrogates; the whole grid takes a few minutes.

    In [10]:
    Copied! 
    def apmsd_parameter_space(data, true_network, p_sl=0.1, levels=np.linspace(0, 1, 11), rng=None):
    # TPR and FPR for every (pc_amplitude, pc_phase) on the grid; [i, j] is
    # pc_amplitude = levels[i], pc_phase = levels[j]
    rng = np.random.default_rng(rng)
    tpr = np.zeros((len(levels), len(levels)))
    fpr = np.zeros_like(tpr)
    for i, pc1 in enumerate(levels):
        for j, pc2 in enumerate(levels):
            _, _, rates = cnd.mir_surrogate_test(
                data, p_sl=p_sl, true_network=true_network,
                pc_amplitude=pc1, pc_phase=pc2, rng=rng,
            )
            tpr[i, j], fpr[i, j] = rates[0, 1:]
    return tpr, fpr


def plot_parameter_space(tpr, fpr, levels=np.linspace(0, 1, 11), title=None, ax=None):
    # Colour: TPR. Red circles: FPR > 0 (area grows with FPR). Green: perfect inference.
    ax = ax or plt.gca()
    pct = 100 * levels
    image = ax.pcolormesh(pct, pct, tpr.T, cmap="viridis", vmin=0, vmax=1, shading="nearest")
    P1, P2 = np.meshgrid(pct, pct, indexing="ij")
    wrong = fpr > 0
    ax.scatter(P1[wrong], P2[wrong], s=400 * fpr[wrong], facecolors="none", edgecolors="red", linewidths=1.5)
    perfect = (tpr == 1) & (fpr == 0)
    ax.scatter(P1[perfect], P2[perfect], s=60, color="limegreen", edgecolors="black")
    ax.set_xlabel("$pc_{1}$, amplitudes randomised (%)")
    ax.set_ylabel("$pc_{2}$, phases randomised (%)")
    ax.set_xticks(pct[::2])
    ax.set_yticks(pct[::2])
    ax.set_aspect("equal")
    if title:
        ax.set_title(title)
    return image

    Choosing the best pair¶

    With a known network, the paper picks the pair whose (TPR, FPR) point is closest to perfect inference, \((1, 0)\), on the ROC plane: \(\tilde d = \sqrt{(1 - \mathrm{TPR})^2 + \mathrm{FPR}^2}\). Here many pairs reach \(\tilde d = 0\), so the first one found is reported.

    Down-sampling (Fig. 3)¶

    The paper also asks how few data points the method needs. If the \(10^5\) points were recorded over 10 seconds, the sampling rate is 10 kHz. Keeping every 100th point gives a 100 Hz recording of 1000 points, and every 500th point a 20 Hz recording of 200 points.

    At 100 Hz (1000 points) many pairs of percentages still recover the chain exactly. At 20 Hz (200 points) none do here, while Fig. 3c of the paper shows success at 20 Hz and even 10 Hz. Taking every 500th point of a chaotic map leaves almost independent samples, and with only 200 of them the MIR estimates are too noisy: the MIR of the linked pairs is no longer clearly above that of their surrogates, so their \(p\)-values are not 0. The paper may have down-sampled differently; the down-sampling script is not in the thesis code, so this part is not a like-for-like comparison.

    Summary¶

    • MIR finds the structure of this network where Pearson correlation fails.
    • APMSD surrogates replace the threshold with a hypothesis test: at \((pc_{1}, pc_{2}) = (0, 60)\%\) the test recovers the chain exactly, as in the paper.
    • The parameter space shows which mix of amplitude and phase randomisation removes the dependence between linked nodes. Here phase randomisation is what matters, which fits the strong phase synchronisation (\(\rho \approx 0.93\)) and weak correlation of this system.
    • The method still works on down-sampled data of 1000 points; at 200 points this implementation does not reproduce the paper's Fig. 3c.

    The functions used are documented in the Network Inference, Surrogate Data and Synchronisation API pages.
